Playworld is an independent reading resource about traditional games and play from different cultures. We introduce board games, street games, kite flying and team games from around the globe, and share easy ways to try them at home, at school and with friends.

Travel the world of play

What Playworld is about

Children and adults everywhere have always found ways to play. Long before screens, people marked boards in the dirt, flew kites on festival days, rolled marbles in the street and invented team games for the village square. Many of these games are still played today, often with small changes from place to place.

Playworld was created to celebrate this shared human habit. Our articles describe traditional games in plain language, explain what each one practises and suggest simple ways to try them. We are a reading resource only and do not sell games, run events or take part in competitions.

A tour of play around the world

These examples show how widely traditional play is shared. Rules and names often differ by region, so treat each description as a general introduction.

South Asia

Carrom and Ludo

Carrom is played by flicking discs across a square board into corner pockets. Ludo comes from an older Indian race game called Pachisi, in which players move pieces around a track.

South Asia

Kabaddi

A fast team game in which a raider tries to tag opponents and return to their own half, all while holding a single breath and chanting.

Africa and Asia

Mancala games

A family of games played by sowing seeds or stones around rows of pits. They are played in many regions under different names.

East Asia

Go and kite flying

Go is a strategy board game of surrounding territory with black and white stones. Kite flying is also a long-standing festival tradition in many East Asian countries.

Southeast Asia

Sepak takraw

Sometimes described as volleyball played with the feet, it features acrobatic kicks of a woven ball over a net.

Europe and beyond

Marbles and hopscotch

Rolling and flicking marbles or hopping through chalk squares are street games enjoyed by children in countless towns around the world.

The Americas

Street and yard games

Games such as tag, jump rope, stickball and hide and seek vary by neighbourhood, with rhymes and rules passed from child to child.

Middle East

Backgammon family

Race games played with dice and checkers on a board of points are popular in cafes and homes across the region and beyond.

Everywhere

Clapping and rhyme games

Hand-clapping songs, counting rhymes and string figures appear in almost every culture and need nothing but people.

Four steps to try a new game from another culture

You do not need special equipment. Most traditional games can be played with simple materials you already have.

1

Pick a game

Choose one that suits the players' ages and the space available, such as hopscotch outdoors or mancala at the table.

2

Learn the rules together

Read a simple description or ask someone who knows the game. Agree on the rules before you start.

3

Make your own set

Draw a board on paper, use buttons or stones as pieces and chalk for squares. Making the set is part of the fun.

4

Talk about where it comes from

Find out how people in other places play it and what it is called. This turns a game into a small cultural journey.

Why playing across cultures matters

Traditional games are a window into how people live and what they value. Learning one invites curiosity about the place it comes from, its language and its customs. For children, it can show that people everywhere share the same love of play.

They also keep generations connected. A grandparent teaching a childhood game or a classmate sharing a favourite from home are small acts that make people feel welcome. Because the rules are often flexible, these games are easy to adapt so that everyone can join in.

Good habits for shared play

  • Respect how each community plays its own game
  • Let players of every age have a role
  • Keep the focus on fun rather than winning
  • Choose a safe space for active games
  • Supervise young children, especially with small pieces

Frequently asked questions

Is Playworld a game store or an events organiser?

No. Playworld does not sell games, run events or organise competitions. It is a reading resource that introduces traditional games in simple language.

Are the rules on this site official?

No. Traditional games have many local variations, so our descriptions are general introductions. Agree on the rules you will use before you begin.

Which games are good for young children?

Hopscotch, hand-clapping games, simple race games and counting games work well. Avoid small pieces for very young children who might put them in their mouths.

Can these games be used in a classroom?

Yes. Many teachers use traditional games to practise counting, language and teamwork and to learn about different cultures.

How can I find out more about a game's origin?

Ask family and community members, check libraries and reliable reference sources, and remember that many games developed in several places over a long time.
